How it works

A QR code is your link plus error correction, and both are fixed by the link — with one loophole. After the link and a short end marker, every remaining data bit is free: scanners stop reading at the marker. QR error correction is linear, so every dot in the code is an XOR of those free bits, and Gaussian elimination can pick them so that as many dots as possible match your drawing — the important ones first: outlines, thin lines, eyes. Nothing is damaged on purpose; the result is an ordinary, error-free QR code.

The dots that can’t be steered — the link itself and part of the error correction — are painted in your drawing’s colour with a small centre square in their true colour. Scanners read the centre of each dot, so the code keeps working while looking like the picture. Blend sets how small those centres get.

The ideas come from Russ Cox’s QArt Codes (2012) and Chu et al.’s Halftone QR Codes (SIGGRAPH Asia 2013).

Tips

  • Shorter links leave more free bits, so the picture comes out cleaner — a link shortener helps.
  • The three corner squares and the dotted lines between them belong to the QR. Draw around the tinted spots.
  • Outlines and details (eyes, edges) survive better than big solid areas, and they scan better too.
  • Test the downloaded code with your phone before printing, keep the white border, and print it at least 3 cm (1.2 in) wide — larger if you slide Blend toward Picture.

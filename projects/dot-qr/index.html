Dot QR

Type a link, draw a small black-and-white picture, and get a QR code with your drawing sitting in the middle — untouched, and still scannable. Everything runs in your browser.

How it works

A QR code is your link plus error correction, and both are fixed by the link — with one loophole. After the link and a short end marker, every remaining data bit is free: scanners stop reading at the marker. QR error correction is linear, so every dot in the code is an XOR of those free bits, and a little linear algebra (Gaussian elimination) can choose them so that every dot in the middle of the code shows your drawing exactly. Nothing is covered up or damaged; it is an ordinary, error-free QR code that happens to contain your picture.

Two things have to stay out of the drawing’s way: the code’s own markers (the three corner squares and the dotted lines between them) and the dots that spell out the link itself, which can’t be changed. Dot QR picks the smallest code where both miss the middle, turning the code a quarter turn if that helps. Bigger codes have one small alignment square at the very centre; scanners don’t need it, so your drawing simply covers it.

The idea comes from Russ Cox’s QArt Codes (2012).

Tips

  • Shorter links leave more room: about 30 characters fits a full 32 × 32 drawing, 45 characters about 20 × 20. A link shortener helps a lot.
  • Smaller drawings make smaller codes, and smaller codes are easier to scan from a distance.
  • Codes with a drawing are fairly dense (often 49 × 49 dots or more), so print them at least 4 cm (1.6 in) wide, keep the white border, and test the download with your phone first.
